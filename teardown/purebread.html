Why this gap is the expensive one

These aren’t cold visitors. They’re your warmest buyers.

It’s easy to file abandoned carts under “people who weren’t going to buy.” That’s the opposite of what they are. Someone who reaches your checkout has already done every hard thing you spend money to make happen:

They found you. They liked the brand enough to browse. They picked a specific piece in a specific colourway. They accepted the price. They started typing their personal details into a form. Then they got distracted, or hesitated for ten seconds, and closed the tab.

Across ecommerce, roughly 7 in 10 carts end that way, and the reasons are almost never “I changed my mind about the product.” They’re shipping cost appearing late, an unexpected step, a payment method that isn’t there — or, most often, simply life interrupting at the wrong moment.

You have already paid for every one of these people. The ad, the post, the drop, the years of building something people want — all of it is already spent by the time someone reaches checkout. Recovering them is the cheapest revenue in the entire store, and it’s usually the least worked.

How recovery actually works

It’s not one reminder. It’s three messages with three different jobs.

This is where most brands that do something still leave the money behind. They send one generic email a day later that says “you left something in your cart” — the same message whether you dropped a $60 tee or a $200 set. It ticks a box. It doesn’t go and get the cart back.

A sequence that works is timed, and each touch does a different job:

Touch one — about an hour later

The nudge

They’re probably still on their phone. This one assumes nothing is wrong — it just puts the exact item back in front of them, by name and colourway, with a one-tap route back to a pre-filled checkout.

Still thinking about the Red / White 2 Stripe hoodie? It’s held for you — here’s your cart, same as you left it.
Touch two — six to eight hours later

The objection

If they didn’t come back, something stopped them. This touch answers the most likely reason before they have to ask — sizing, shipping cost and timeline, returns. Not a discount. Just the information that was missing at the moment they hesitated.

Sizing runs true — if it’s not right we’ll swap it. Shipping’s [X], arrives in [Y] days.
Touch three — around 24 hours

The close

The last honest reason to act now. For a brand that drops in limited runs, that reason already exists and doesn’t need inventing — colourways sell through, and the one they picked may not be there next week. Truth converts better than a discount, and it doesn’t train people to wait for one.

Red / White is moving — didn’t want you to miss the size you had.

Three touches, spread across a day and a half, each one naming the actual piece they left. That’s the whole mechanism. It isn’t clever — it’s just done properly and on time, every single time, without anyone having to remember to do it.

The part most brands miss

A perfect message nobody opens is worth nothing

Brands spend their energy on what the recovery message says. The bigger lever is whether it gets seen at all — and that’s decided by the channel, before a single word is read.

Email — typical open rate
~20%

Four out of five people never see the message you wrote. It isn’t rejected. It’s unopened.

WhatsApp — typical open rate
~95%

It lands where they already talk to people. It gets read, usually within minutes.

This is why email-only recovery tends to claw back only a few percent of abandoned revenue, while email and WhatsApp layered together — the same three touches, split across both channels so they never double up — lands in a completely different range.

Same customers. Same carts. Same messages. The only thing that changed is whether anyone saw them.
